6. Sprites

^ Back to top

A sprite is a small bitmap — a player's ship, an enemy, a bullet, a cursor — that can be positioned and moved independently anywhere on the screen. On the ZX Spectrum every graphic is ultimately composed of individual bits in the display file, so drawing a sprite means copying a block of pre-defined pixel bytes into the correct display-file addresses. This chapter builds three library routines that make sprite drawing practical, starting from the simplest possible case and working up through transparency masking and safe screen-edge clipping.

Defining Sprite Data

The Sprite Descriptor

A sprite is described by a small block of bytes assembled into the program binary with DB directives. The first two bytes form a header that records the dimensions; the remaining bytes are the pixel data, stored row by row from top to bottom.

; Unmasked sprite layout
sprite_name:
    DB height        ; number of pixel rows           (1–191)
    DB width_bytes   ; display bytes per row          (1–32)
    DB ...           ; pixel data, row-major order
                    ; bit 7 of each byte = left-most pixel

The bit ordering follows the same convention as the display file itself: bit 7 (the most-significant bit) represents the left-most pixel of the byte, and bit 0 the right-most. A width_bytes of 1 gives an 8-pixel-wide sprite; 2 gives 16 pixels, and so on up to the full 32-byte row width of the screen.

An 8×8 Example: the Alien

The following sprite represents a classic arcade-style alien invader. Each row is a single byte; dots mark cleared bits (paper) and asterisks mark set bits (ink).

alien_sprite:
    DB 8, 1               ; 8 rows tall, 1 byte (8 pixels) wide
    ;      7 6 5 4 3 2 1 0
    DB %00100100          ; . . * . . * . .   antennae
    DB %01111110          ; . * * * * * * .   head
    DB %11011011          ; * * . * * . * *   eyes
    DB %11111111          ; * * * * * * * *   body
    DB %10111101          ; * . * * * * . *   arms
    DB %01011010          ; . * . * * . * .   waist
    DB %01000010          ; . * . . . . * .   legs
    DB %00100100          ; . . * . . * . .   feet

To visualise the sprite, read each byte from left to right, substituting * for 1 and . for 0. Reading the table above row by row produces the alien shape.

A 16×5 Example: the Spaceship

When width_bytes is greater than 1, each logical row of the sprite is stored as consecutive bytes, left byte first. A 16-pixel-wide sprite has two bytes per row; the draw routine writes them one after the other into consecutive display addresses on the same scan line.

ship_sprite:
    DB 5, 2               ; 5 rows tall, 2 bytes (16 pixels) wide
    ;      byte 0           byte 1
    ;      7 6 5 4 3 2 1 0  7 6 5 4 3 2 1 0
    DB %00000001, %10000000  ; . . . . . . . * * . . . . . . .   nose
    DB %00011111, %11111000  ; . . . * * * * * * * * * * . . .   fuselage
    DB %11111111, %11111111  ; * * * * * * * * * * * * * * * *   widest
    DB %00011111, %11111000  ; . . . * * * * * * * * * * . . .   fuselage
    DB %00000001, %10000000  ; . . . . . . . * * . . . . . . .   tail

Character Alignment

The routines built in this chapter require the sprite's x coordinate to be a multiple of 8 — that is, aligned to a display-file byte boundary. This is called character alignment. With alignment enforced, each sprite byte maps directly onto one display-file byte with no bit-shifting at all, keeping the drawing code simple and fast.

Pixel-precise positioning at arbitrary x coordinates requires shifting every sprite byte by the sub-character offset before writing, producing one or two output bytes per source byte. That technique — while essential for smooth animation — adds significant complexity and is beyond the scope of this chapter; the routines here use the character-aligned approach that was standard in many early Spectrum games.

Drawing Sprites

The IX Register

The existing library routines pass all parameters in the B, C, D, E register pairs. A sprite drawing routine needs a third input: a pointer to the sprite data. Neither the 8-bit registers nor HL (which calc_pixel_addr overwrites with the display address) are available, so this chapter introduces IX.

IX is one of the Z80's two 16-bit index registers. Like HL it holds a 16-bit address, but it also supports a displacement: (IX+n) reads from the byte at address IX+n without changing IX itself. This makes IX ideal for walking through structured data — read the header at offsets 0 and 1, then advance IX past the header with two INC IX instructions to point at the pixel data.

InstructionMeaning
LD A, (IX+0)Load A from address IX+0 (the byte IX points at)
LD A, (IX+1)Load A from the next byte without touching IX
INC IXAdvance IX by one (full 16-bit increment)
LD (IX+0), AStore A at the address IX points at

The Two-Loop Pattern

Drawing a rectangular sprite requires two nested loops. The outer loop counts pixel rows from top to bottom. The inner loop copies one row's worth of bytes from the sprite data into consecutive display-file addresses on the same scan line.

outer: PUSH HL              ; save start-of-row display address
       ; inner: write width_bytes from IX into HL, then INC IX and INC L
       POP  HL              ; restore start-of-row (L = original column)
       ; advance HL to the next pixel row
       ; decrement row counter; loop if not zero

The PUSH/POP pair is essential. The inner loop increments L to step across the byte columns of the current scan line. After the inner loop, L has advanced by width_bytes from where it started. Restoring HL via POP brings L back to the original column value so that the row-advance arithmetic (which affects both H and L) operates on the correct starting address.

The row-advance logic is identical to the dv_cross path in draw_vline: check whether the scan-line field (H bits 2:0) has reached 7; if not, INC H; if so, clear LLL and step the character-row field in L, propagating any carry into the screen-third field in H.

One further point on INC L versus INC HL: within a single scan line, all display bytes share the same H value. Incrementing only L steps one byte to the right without disturbing the H field that encodes the row address. Using INC HL instead would be correct but costs one extra byte and one extra clock; INC L is both shorter and faster, and Chapter 4 established that it is always safe within a row.

draw_sprite.asm

; draw_sprite
; Draw an unmasked, character-aligned sprite.
; Sprite pixels are OR'd into the display — existing set pixels are
; preserved where the sprite byte has a 0 bit; existing clear pixels
; are forced on where the sprite byte has a 1 bit.
;
; Input:  B  = y          (pixel row,    0–191)
;         C  = x          (pixel column, must be a multiple of 8)
;         IX = pointer to sprite descriptor:
;                DB height       ; number of pixel rows  (1–191)
;                DB width_bytes  ; bytes per row          (1–32)
;                DB ...          ; pixel data, row-major
; Destroys: A, B, C, D, E, H, L, IX
draw_sprite:
    LD   A, (IX+0)         ; read height from sprite header
    LD   (ds_rows), A      ; store as decreasing row counter
    LD   A, (IX+1)         ; read byte-width from sprite header
    LD   (ds_width), A
    INC  IX                ; advance past height byte
    INC  IX                ; advance past width byte — IX → first pixel byte

    CALL calc_pixel_addr   ; HL = display address of (y,x)  A = mask (unused)

ds_row_loop:
    PUSH HL                ; save start-of-row display address

    LD   A, (ds_width)
    LD   B, A              ; B = byte counter for this row
ds_col_loop:
    LD   A, (IX+0)         ; fetch next sprite pixel byte from data
    OR   (HL)              ; merge: set sprite 1-bits, keep existing display bits
    LD   (HL), A
    INC  IX                ; advance sprite data pointer
    INC  L                 ; next display byte on same row (INC L safe within a row)
    DJNZ ds_col_loop

    POP  HL                ; restore start-of-row address (L = original column)

    ; — Advance HL to the next pixel row —
    LD   A, H
    AND  $07               ; isolate LLL (scan-line field, H bits 2:0)
    CP   $07               ; last scan line of this character row?
    JR   Z, ds_cross
    INC  H                 ; no: just increment the scan-line field
    JR   ds_row_dec

ds_cross:
    ; LLL was 7: wrap scan line to 0, step character-row field RRR in L
    LD   A, H
    AND  $F8               ; clear LLL → scan line resets to 0
    LD   H, A
    LD   A, L
    ADD  A, $20            ; step RRR field (L bits 7:5 = char row within third)
    LD   L, A
    JR   NC, ds_row_dec   ; no carry: still in the same screen third
    LD   A, H
    ADD  A, $08            ; carry: step TT field (H bits 4:3) — next screen third
    LD   H, A

ds_row_dec:
    LD   A, (ds_rows)
    DEC  A
    LD   (ds_rows), A
    JR   NZ, ds_row_loop
    RET

ds_rows:  DB 0   ; rows remaining (counts down)
ds_width: DB 0   ; bytes per row (constant for the duration of the call)

Walkthrough

Header read and IX advance. The two LD A, (IX+n) instructions extract height and width from the sprite descriptor and save them in memory variables, then two INC IX instructions advance IX from the header to the first pixel byte. Because calc_pixel_addr uses and destroys B, reading the header before the call lets us pass B = y and C = x to the routine unmodified.

calc_pixel_addr. Called with B = y and C = x exactly as in every other library routine. Since x is a multiple of 8, the bitmask in A on return is always %10000000 (the leftmost bit), which the routine discards — we are writing whole bytes, not individual pixels.

Inner column loop. LD A, (IX+0) fetches the current sprite byte; OR (HL) combines it with whatever is already in the display (preserving all set bits in both the sprite and the background); LD (HL), A writes the result back. INC IX advances through the sprite data and INC L steps one byte to the right in the display. After width_bytes iterations, DJNZ falls through with IX pointing at the first byte of the next sprite row.

Row advance. After the inner loop, POP HL restores HL to the start-of-row address. Testing H bits 2:0 against 7 selects the fast path (INC H) or the wrap path (AND $F8 + ADD L, $20 + optional ADD H, $08). The full explanation of this arithmetic appears in Chapter 4's vertical-line section.

Example Usage

    ; Draw the alien at pixel row 64, column 80
    LD   B, 64             ; y = 64  (row 8 of the middle screen third)
    LD   C, 80             ; x = 80  (byte column 10, multiple of 8)
    LD   IX, alien_sprite
    CALL draw_sprite

    ; Draw the ship at pixel row 100, column 120
    LD   B, 100
    LD   C, 120            ; x = 120 = 15 × 8 ✓
    LD   IX, ship_sprite
    CALL draw_sprite

Both calls require calc_pixel_addr to be assembled into the same binary. No other library routine is needed.

Masking & Transparency

The Limitation of OR-Drawing

draw_sprite OR-merges every sprite byte into the display. This means sprite 1-bits always set the corresponding display pixel regardless of what was already there — the sprite's solid pixels appear correctly. However, sprite 0-bits leave the existing display pixel untouched: they are transparent. This sounds useful, but it creates two problems in practice.

First, when a sprite is moved, OR-drawing the new position cannot erase the old position: the old pixels are set and nothing in the new draw can clear them. The screen accumulates a trail of ghost images.

Second, OR-transparency means the sprite's "paper" colour (its background within the bounding rectangle) shows through as whatever the display happens to contain. A sprite that is supposed to have a solid coloured body looks hollow whenever it passes over existing graphics.

Both problems require the ability to clear display pixels within the sprite's opaque area as well as set them. That is what a mask achieves.

The AND-OR Technique

For each display byte covered by the sprite, three values are involved: the existing display byte, an AND mask byte, and an OR pixel byte. The update is:

display = (display AND mask_byte) OR pixel_byte

The AND mask uses a 0 bit to mean opaque (belonging to the sprite) and a 1 bit to mean transparent (background shows through). The OR pixel byte then provides the actual ink/paper pattern within the opaque area.

AND bitOR bitResultMeaning
011Sprite ink pixel
000Sprite paper pixel (background erased)
10bg bit unchangedTransparent — background preserved

The AND pass punches a hole exactly the shape of the opaque area, clearing all those display bits to zero. The OR pass then paints the sprite's ink pixels into that hole. The result is a clean, opaque sprite that fully replaces whatever was beneath it in its opaque region, while leaving the rest of the display untouched.

The Masked Sprite Format

The masked sprite descriptor uses the same header as the unmasked format, but each pixel byte is paired with a preceding AND mask byte. For a sprite with width_bytes of N, each row in the data section contains N AND bytes followed by N OR bytes — grouped as pairs, one pair per byte column, reading left to right:

masked_sprite_name:
    DB height
    DB width_bytes
    ; for each row, interleaved AND/OR pairs left-to-right:
    DB and_col0, or_col0 [, and_col1, or_col1, ...]   ; row 0
    DB ...                                            ; row 1 …

The total data size for a masked sprite is 2 + height × width_bytes × 2 bytes (twice the pixel data of an unmasked sprite of the same dimensions).

Here is the alien sprite redefined with a mask. The AND mask 0-bits enclose the alien's bounding silhouette; the OR byte provides the actual pixel pattern within that silhouette.

alien_masked:
    DB 8, 1                          ; 8 rows, 1 byte wide
    ;      AND mask    OR pixels
    ;      7 6 5 4 3 2 1 0   7 6 5 4 3 2 1 0
    DB %11011011, %00100100          ; opaque: bits 5 and 2 only  (antennae)
    DB %10000001, %01111110          ; opaque: bits 6:1            (head)
    DB %00000000, %11011011          ; fully opaque                (eyes)
    DB %00000000, %11111111          ; fully opaque                (body)
    DB %00000000, %10111101          ; fully opaque                (arms)
    DB %00000000, %01011010          ; fully opaque                (waist)
    DB %10000001, %01000010          ; opaque: bits 6:1            (legs)
    DB %11011011, %00100100          ; opaque: bits 5 and 2 only  (feet)

Notice that for the four fully-opaque body rows the AND mask is $00. Every display bit in those byte positions is cleared before the OR pixel byte is applied, so the sprite's paper colour (0 bits in the OR byte) genuinely erases the background.

draw_sprite_masked.asm

; draw_sprite_masked
; Draw a masked, character-aligned sprite.
; For every byte position: display = (display AND mask_byte) OR pixel_byte
;
; AND mask:  bit = 0 → opaque   (sprite owns this bit)
;            bit = 1 → transparent (background preserved)
; OR pixel:  within opaque bits, 1 = ink, 0 = paper
;
; Input:  B  = y  (pixel row,    0–191)
;         C  = x  (pixel column, must be a multiple of 8)
;         IX = pointer to masked sprite descriptor
; Destroys: A, B, C, D, E, H, L, IX
draw_sprite_masked:
    LD   A, (IX+0)
    LD   (dm_rows), A
    LD   A, (IX+1)
    LD   (dm_width), A
    INC  IX
    INC  IX                ; IX → first AND byte of row 0

    CALL calc_pixel_addr   ; HL = display address of (y,x)

dm_row_loop:
    PUSH HL
    LD   A, (dm_width)
    LD   B, A
dm_col_loop:
    LD   A, (IX+0)         ; AND mask byte: 0 = opaque, 1 = transparent
    AND  (HL)              ; punch hole: clear opaque sprite area in display
    LD   D, A              ; save masked display value in D
    INC  IX                ; advance to the paired OR pixel byte
    LD   A, (IX+0)         ; OR pixel byte
    OR   D                 ; paint sprite into the hole
    LD   (HL), A           ; write result
    INC  IX                ; advance to AND byte of next column
    INC  L
    DJNZ dm_col_loop

    POP  HL

    ; — Advance HL to the next pixel row —
    LD   A, H
    AND  $07
    CP   $07
    JR   Z, dm_cross
    INC  H
    JR   dm_row_dec
dm_cross:
    LD   A, H
    AND  $F8
    LD   H, A
    LD   A, L
    ADD  A, $20
    LD   L, A
    JR   NC, dm_row_dec
    LD   A, H
    ADD  A, $08
    LD   H, A
dm_row_dec:
    LD   A, (dm_rows)
    DEC  A
    LD   (dm_rows), A
    JR   NZ, dm_row_loop
    RET

dm_rows:  DB 0
dm_width: DB 0

Walkthrough: the Inner Column Loop

The key is the use of D as a temporary. The inner loop body is:

    LD  A, (IX+0)   ; A = AND mask
    AND (HL)        ; A = display & mask  (opaque area cleared)
    LD  D, A        ; stash the cleared-display value
    INC IX          ; IX now points to the OR pixel byte
    LD  A, (IX+0)   ; A = OR pixel
    OR  D           ; A = (display & mask) | pixel
    LD  (HL), A     ; write the result
    INC IX          ; advance to AND byte of next column
    INC L

D holds the intermediate value — the display byte after the AND mask has punched the hole — between the two INC IX instructions. After the OR D, the accumulated result is the final pixel value for that byte position. The DJNZ then repeats for the next byte column, where IX now points at the next AND byte. The register D is available because the row loop does not otherwise use it; E and the rest of the registers are either the loop counter (B via DJNZ) or the addresses (H, L, IX).

Erasing Sprites

A masked sprite can be erased cleanly only if the background it was drawn over is known. Two practical approaches cover most Spectrum games.

XOR erase (unmasked sprites only). When draw_sprite OR-draws a sprite over a blank background (all display bytes zero in that region), XOR-ing those same sprite bytes back removes them: every bit that was set by OR is cleared by XOR. Chapter 3's toggle_pixel demonstrates the principle — XOR with a value once to draw; XOR again with the same value to erase. This works only when the background is guaranteed clear. A simple XOR variant of draw_sprite replaces OR (HL) with XOR (HL).

Background save and restore. Before drawing, copy the display bytes that the sprite will overwrite into a save buffer the same size as the sprite's pixel data. To erase, copy the save buffer back. This is the robust general solution and underpins the double-buffering technique covered in Chapter 9.

; XOR-erase variant of draw_sprite
; Call once to draw; call again at the same position to erase.
; Input/destroys: same as draw_sprite
draw_sprite_xor:
    LD   A, (IX+0)
    LD   (ds_rows), A
    LD   A, (IX+1)
    LD   (ds_width), A
    INC  IX
    INC  IX
    CALL calc_pixel_addr
dsx_row_loop:
    PUSH HL
    LD   A, (ds_width)
    LD   B, A
dsx_col_loop:
    LD   A, (IX+0)
    XOR  (HL)              ; toggle instead of set — call twice to restore
    LD   (HL), A
    INC  IX
    INC  L
    DJNZ dsx_col_loop
    POP  HL
    ; … row-advance logic identical to draw_sprite …

Clipping at Screen Edges

The Corruption Problem

The display file occupies addresses $4000–$57FF. The attribute file follows immediately at $5800–$5AFF. If a sprite write strays below pixel row 191 the routine advances HL into the attribute file and overwrites colour data with pixel bytes — corrupting colours but not crashing the machine. Writing further still reaches general RAM. Neither draw_sprite nor draw_sprite_masked performs any boundary check, so callers must guarantee the sprite fits entirely on-screen.

There are two practical strategies. The first is a pre-clip guard that simply refuses to draw the sprite if any part would land off-screen. The second is partial clipping that reduces the effective height and width to only the visible portion. The library provides both: a guard is easily written by the caller; draw_sprite_clipped implements partial clipping for the bottom and right edges.

Understanding the Clip Boundaries

For a character-aligned sprite starting at (y, x):

EdgeOff-screen conditionClipped value
Bottomy + height > 192visible_rows = 192 − y
Rightx/8 + width_bytes > 32visible_cols = 32 − x/8

Left and top clipping require the x or y start position to be negative — outside the range of an unsigned byte. The routines in this book treat all coordinates as unsigned 8-bit values (0–255), so negative positions cannot be expressed. Games that need left or top clipping typically manage it by storing sprite positions as signed 16-bit values and computing the starting offset into the sprite data before calling the draw routine; that technique is not covered here.

Bottom Clipping: Capping the Row Count

The bottom clip is computed with a single comparison. If the sprite fits, the full height is used; if not, the available number of rows (192 − y) is used instead. In Z80 this is a subtraction followed by a comparison:

    LD   A, (IX+0)         ; full sprite height → D
    LD   D, A
    LD   A, 192
    SUB  B                 ; available rows = 192 - y  (B = y)
    CP   D                 ; available >= height?
    JR   NC, .h_ok         ; yes: use the full height
    LD   D, A              ; no:  use available rows
.h_ok:
    LD   (dsc_rows), D     ; store the clipped row count

The CP instruction sets carry if A (available) is less than D (height). If carry is clear, there is enough room; if carry is set, the available count in A is the clip limit.

Right Clipping: Capping the Byte Count

Byte-column arithmetic operates in the range 0–31. The starting byte column is x / 8 (three right rotations and an AND to discard the garbage rotated in). Available columns to the right are 32 − start_col. If the sprite's width_bytes exceeds that, it is capped.

    LD   A, (IX+1)         ; full sprite width in bytes → E
    LD   E, A
    LD   A, C              ; C = x
    RRCA
    RRCA
    RRCA
    AND  $1F               ; start byte-column = x / 8
    LD   D, A              ; D = start column
    LD   A, 32
    SUB  D                 ; available byte-columns to the right
    CP   E                 ; available >= width?
    JR   NC, .w_ok
    ; clip: record how many bytes to skip per row so IX stays in sync
    LD   (dsc_width), A    ; visible byte-columns
    LD   D, A
    LD   A, E
    SUB  D                 ; skip = full_width - clipped_width
    LD   (dsc_skip), A
    JR   .draw
.w_ok:
    LD   (dsc_width), E    ; use full width
    XOR  A
    LD   (dsc_skip), A    ; nothing to skip

The dsc_skip variable is the key difference from plain draw_sprite. When the sprite is wider than the visible area, the column loop writes only dsc_width bytes per row. To keep IX aligned with the sprite data for the next row, the skip loop must advance IX by dsc_skip additional steps before the row-advance logic runs. Without this, every subsequent row would read from the wrong position in the sprite data.

draw_sprite_clipped.asm

; draw_sprite_clipped
; Draw a character-aligned sprite with bottom and right edge clipping.
; Rows that would fall below y=191 and byte-columns that would extend past
; column 31 are silently trimmed before drawing begins.
; If y >= 192 the sprite is entirely off-screen and is not drawn.
; Left and top edge clipping are not supported: x must be 0–248 (multiple of
; 8) and y must be 0–191 before calling.
;
; Input:  B  = y  (pixel row,    0–191)
;         C  = x  (pixel column, multiple of 8, 0–248)
;         IX = pointer to sprite descriptor (same format as draw_sprite)
; Destroys: A, B, C, D, E, H, L, IX
draw_sprite_clipped:
    ; — Return immediately if the sprite starts below the screen —
    LD   A, B
    CP   192
    RET  NC

    ; — Clip height: limit to rows that fit below y —
    LD   A, (IX+0)         ; full sprite height
    LD   D, A              ; D = full height
    LD   A, 192
    SUB  B                 ; available rows from y to the screen bottom (192 - y)
    CP   D                 ; available >= full height?
    JR   NC, dsc_h_ok
    LD   D, A              ; no: use only the available rows
dsc_h_ok:
    LD   A, D
    LD   (dsc_rows), A

    ; — Clip width: limit to byte-columns that fit to the right of x —
    LD   A, (IX+1)         ; full sprite width in bytes
    LD   E, A              ; E = full width (kept for skip-count calculation below)
    LD   A, C
    RRCA
    RRCA
    RRCA
    AND  $1F               ; start byte-column = x / 8  (0–31)
    LD   D, A
    LD   A, 32
    SUB  D                 ; byte-columns available to the right of start_col
    CP   E                 ; available >= full width?
    JR   NC, dsc_w_ok
    LD   (dsc_width), A    ; clip: store only the available column count
    LD   D, A              ; D = clipped width
    LD   A, E
    SUB  D                 ; skip = full_width - clipped_width (bytes per row to skip)
    LD   (dsc_skip), A
    JR   dsc_draw
dsc_w_ok:
    LD   A, E
    LD   (dsc_width), A
    XOR  A
    LD   (dsc_skip), A     ; no bytes to skip on the right

dsc_draw:
    LD   A, (dsc_rows)
    OR   A
    RET  Z                 ; zero rows after clipping: nothing to draw
    INC  IX                ; skip height byte in header
    INC  IX                ; skip width byte  in header — IX → first pixel byte
    CALL calc_pixel_addr   ; HL = display-file address of (y, x)

dsc_row_loop:
    PUSH HL                ; save start-of-row display address

    ; — Draw one row's visible bytes —
    LD   A, (dsc_width)
    LD   B, A
dsc_col_loop:
    LD   A, (IX+0)
    OR   (HL)
    LD   (HL), A
    INC  IX
    INC  L
    DJNZ dsc_col_loop

    ; — Advance IX past the right-clipped bytes so it reaches the next row —
    LD   A, (dsc_skip)
    OR   A
    JR   Z, dsc_row_adv    ; nothing clipped: no skip needed
    LD   B, A
dsc_skip_loop:
    INC  IX
    DJNZ dsc_skip_loop

dsc_row_adv:
    POP  HL                ; restore start-of-row address

    ; — Advance HL to the next pixel row —
    LD   A, H
    AND  $07               ; isolate LLL (scan-line field)
    CP   $07
    JR   Z, dsc_cross
    INC  H
    JR   dsc_row_dec
dsc_cross:
    LD   A, H
    AND  $F8               ; clear LLL
    LD   H, A
    LD   A, L
    ADD  A, $20            ; step RRR (character-row field)
    LD   L, A
    JR   NC, dsc_row_dec
    LD   A, H
    ADD  A, $08            ; step TT (screen-third field)
    LD   H, A
dsc_row_dec:
    LD   A, (dsc_rows)
    DEC  A
    LD   (dsc_rows), A
    JR   NZ, dsc_row_loop
    RET

dsc_rows:  DB 0            ; row count after bottom clipping
dsc_width: DB 0            ; byte-columns per row after right clipping
dsc_skip:  DB 0            ; bytes to advance IX past at end of each row

The Skip Loop

The skip loop — LD B, A followed by DJNZ dsc_skip_loop — is only entered when dsc_skip is non-zero. The OR A test before it sets the zero flag without changing A, allowing the JR Z to bypass the entire loop for unclipped rows. When the loop does run, it simply performs dsc_skip additional INC IX steps, discarding those sprite bytes, so that IX arrives at the correct position — the start of the next sprite row — before the row-advance logic is applied.

A Guard for the Left and Top Edges

When a sprite could potentially start off the left or top edge, the caller must ensure valid coordinates before calling any draw routine. A simple guard might look like this:

    ; Reject the draw if y or x would place the sprite's top-left off-screen.
    ; (Left and top clipping are not supported by the library routines.)
    LD   A, B
    CP   192
    RET  NC              ; y off the bottom (draw_sprite_clipped handles partial)
    LD   A, C
    AND  %00000111
    JR   NZ, .skip     ; x not byte-aligned: skip this sprite
    CALL draw_sprite_clipped
.skip:

Static Decoration with draw_sprite

The three bunkers are drawn once during setup, before main_loop begins, with no bookkeeping and no erase. draw_sprite is the right tool here because the bunkers are static: the display starts all-black, so a simple OR writes exactly the pixels that should be set with no concern about what was underneath. The three calls are structurally identical — load B with the y row, C with the x column, IX with the sprite address, then call. draw_sprite carries no overhead for masks, skip counts, or clip calculations; it is the fastest of the three routines and appropriate for any sprite that is drawn once and left alone.

The interesting consequence reveals itself later. draw_sprite only adds pixels; it has no way to protect them once drawn. When the invader sweeps through BUNKER_Y = 160, the erase step applies INV_ERASE via draw_sprite_masked. Every AND byte in that sprite is $00, which clears every display byte in the 16‑×‑8 bounding box to zero, regardless of what was already there — including any bunker pixels that happen to fall inside the box. The invader has no knowledge of the bunkers; the erasure is simply the result of a fully-opaque AND mask applied over the same screen area. This is the defining limitation of unmasked drawing: pixels can be added but not selectively protected from future masked operations.

Animated Movement with draw_sprite_masked

The invader uses an erase-then-draw pattern every frame. The erase step calls draw_sprite_masked with INV_ERASE, which has AND = $00 and OR = $00 in every byte. For each display byte the operation is (display AND $00) OR $00 = $00 — an unconditional clear. The entire 16‑×‑8 bounding box is set to paper colour (black) with no regard for any other pixels inside it. The draw step then applies the animated frame data against the guaranteed-clean background.

Animation is produced by toggling inv_frame between 0 and 1 each frame using XOR 1. Frame A has wide legs on row 6 and inner feet on row 7; frame B swaps the two. Rows 0–5 are identical between frames, so only 16 bytes change per cycle. IX is loaded with INV_SPRITE_A or INV_SPRITE_B before the draw call, and draw_sprite_masked reads the frame pointer from IX to traverse whichever data it points to.

Five state variables drive the movement. inv_x and inv_y hold the current top-left pixel coordinate; inv_dir is 0 for rightward and 1 for leftward. Each frame, inv_x is stepped by STEP_X = 8 in the current direction. When x reaches RIGHT_MAX = 240 or underflows below 0, the direction is reversed and inv_step_down adds STEP_Y = 16 to inv_y, wrapping to 0 if the result would exceed BOTTOM_MAX = 184. Clamping at both edges ensures inv_x is always a character-aligned column in the range 0–240, as all three sprite routines require.

The main loop ends with JP main_loop rather than JR main_loop. JR encodes a signed 8-bit displacement and can only reach within ±127 bytes of the following instruction. The UFO-clearing loop added enough code between main_loop and the jump to exceed that range, so JP — which uses a full 16-bit address — is used instead. This is a practical reminder that JR is efficient for short forward or backward branches but must be replaced by JP when the target grows too distant.

UFO Clipping with draw_sprite_clipped

draw_sprite_clipped performs a pure OR-draw with no AND mask, so it cannot erase its own previous frame: ORing zero bits into the display leaves existing pixels unchanged. The solution used here is a clear-before-draw pattern: the entire character row the UFO occupies is zeroed before each frame is drawn, removing any trace of the previous position automatically regardless of where the sprite was.

Character row 1 (pixel rows 8–15) spans eight scan lines. Within the display file, all eight share the same byte-column layout: L runs from $20 (column 0) to $3F (column 31), while H steps from $40 (scan line 0, y=8) to $47 (scan line 7, y=15). The clear loop exploits this regularity directly: it sets H = $40 and an outer counter D = 8, then for each of the eight scan lines it resets L = $20, runs a 32-byte inner DJNZ loop writing $00, and increments H to step to the next scan line. The entire 256-byte row is zeroed in two nested loops with no address arithmetic beyond a single INC H per scan line.

The UFO's x coordinate is stored as a single byte. Each frame STEP_X = 8 is added with an 8-bit ADD A, STEP_X. When x = 248 the next addition produces 256, which the 8-bit register truncates to 0 — the UFO reappears at the left edge automatically, with no comparison or branch required. The full sequence of positions is 0, 8, 16, …, 240, 248, 0, 8, … and the hardware overflow is the wrap.

At x = 248 the 2-byte UFO needs byte columns 31 and 32. Column 32 does not exist. draw_sprite_clipped detects this: it converts x to a byte-column (248 ÷ 8 = 31), finds that only 32 − 31 = 1 byte-column is available while the sprite is 2 bytes wide, and reduces the draw width to 1. The skip count (dsc_skip) is set to 1 so that IX advances past the right-hand byte of each sprite row without writing it. Only the left 8 pixels of the UFO appear at x = 248; the right half is trimmed silently with no caller-side handling.

UFO_Y is set to 8 so that the invader never overlaps the UFO's character row. The invader's y coordinate advances in multiples of STEP_Y = 16 from 0, producing only the values 0, 16, 32, 48, … The value 8 is never in that sequence, so the invader's 8-row bounding box (rows y to y + 7) never covers rows 8–15. The UFO row clear and the invader erase operate on disjoint areas of the display file.

What You See

The screen opens black with three bunker shapes near the bottom at columns 24, 112, and 200. The invader appears at the top-left and moves right one character column per frame, legs alternating between frame A and frame B. A small UFO travels across the second character row. As the UFO approaches the right edge its right half shrinks — at x = 248 only the left 8 pixels of the 16-pixel body remain — then the full shape reappears at the left edge on the next frame as the coordinate wraps through zero.

Each time the invader reverses it steps down two character rows. When it eventually reaches y = 160 — the bunker row — its erase sprite blacks out a 16‑×‑8 box on every frame. Any bunker whose position coincides exactly with the invader's bounding box on that pass has its pixels cleared by the AND = $00 erase, one pass at a time. Bunkers at x = 24, 112, or 200 are erased when the invader is at the same x on its y = 160 sweep; bunkers at other columns survive. The destruction is not programmed explicitly — it is the natural consequence of a fully-opaque masked erase applied over the same screen region as an unmasked OR-draw.

Scrolling the Whole Screen

Scrolling the ZX Spectrum display is not a simple block memory move. The display file occupies $4000–$57FF, but those 6144 bytes are arranged by scan-line offset within character row within screen third, not by visual top-to-bottom order. A naive LDIR from $4020 to $4000 would not move the second character row up to the first — it would shift the second scan line of character row 0 over the first, leaving the remaining 23 rows unchanged.

To scroll correctly we need to understand how H and L encode a display address. For a pixel at visual row y and byte column x:

  • H = $40 | (TT << 3) | LLL — TT selects the screen third (0 = rows 0–63, 1 = rows 64–127, 2 = rows 128–191) and LLL is the scan-line offset within the character row (0–7).
  • L = (RRR << 5) | x — RRR selects the character row within the third (0–7) and x is the byte column (0–31).

The key insight that makes efficient scrolling possible: for a fixed H value (fixed TT and LLL), the eight character rows of that third each contribute a 32-byte block sitting at L = 0, 32, 64, 96, 128, 160, 192, and 224. These eight blocks fill L from $00 to $FF without any gap. That means a single LDIR of 224 bytes can shift seven rows (rows 1–7 of the third) upward into the positions of rows 0–6, all without any inner loop.

The only complication is the boundary between thirds. Character row 8 (first row of TT=1) must move into character row 7's slot (last row of TT=0). Source and destination have different H values, so this 32-byte copy cannot be merged with either adjacent 224-byte block. The same applies to the boundary between TT=1 and TT=2 (rows 16 and 15).

Because the 224-byte within-third LDIR overwrites rows 0–6 using the original data from rows 1–7, the cross-boundary copy (row 8 → row 7) must happen before row 7 is overwritten. The correct per-LLL sequence is therefore: within TT=0, cross TT=0→1, within TT=1, cross TT=1→2, within TT=2, then clear row 23. The outer loop iterates that sequence once for each of the eight scan-line offsets (LLL = 0–7).

Because LDIR destroys BC, the current LLL value cannot be kept in a register across the copies. The routine stores it in a one-byte variable suc_lll and reloads it before each address computation.

scroll_up_char.asm

; scroll_up_char
; Scrolls the entire ZX Spectrum display up by one character row (8 pixels).
; Character row 0 is lost; the new bottom row (char row 23) is filled with
; zeros.  Attributes at $5800–$5AFF are not touched.
; Destroys: A, B, C, D, E, H, L.
scroll_up_char:
    XOR  A
    LD   (suc_lll), A      ; LLL = 0  (scan-line offset counter)
suc_lll_loop:
    ; ── TT=0: copy char rows 1–7 → rows 0–6 (224 bytes) ────────────────
    ; Within H = $40 | LLL, all 8 char rows of the first third sit at
    ; L = 0, 32, 64 … 224.  Copying from L=$20 (row 1) to L=$00 (row 0)
    ; for 224 bytes shifts rows 1–7 into rows 0–6 in one LDIR.
    LD   A, (suc_lll)
    OR   $40               ; H = $40 | LLL  (TT=0, this scan-line offset)
    LD   H, A
    LD   L, $20            ; source: char row 1 (RRR=1, L = 1×32 = $20)
    LD   D, H              ; dest same third and LLL
    LD   E, $00            ; dest: char row 0 (RRR=0, L=$00)
    LD   BC, 224           ; 7 rows × 32 bytes per row
    LDIR

    ; ── Cross TT=0→1: char row 8 → char row 7 ─────────────────────────
    ; Row 8 is the first row of TT=1 (H = $48|LLL, L=$00).
    ; Row 7 is the last row of TT=0  (H = $40|LLL, L = 7×32 = $E0).
    LD   A, (suc_lll)
    OR   $48               ; source H: TT=1, this LLL
    LD   H, A
    LD   L, $00            ; first byte of TT=1 (RRR=0)
    LD   A, (suc_lll)
    OR   $40               ; dest H: TT=0, this LLL
    LD   D, A
    LD   E, $E0            ; dest: char row 7 (RRR=7, L = $E0)
    LD   BC, 32
    LDIR

    ; ── TT=1: copy char rows 9–15 → rows 8–14 (224 bytes) ──────────────
    LD   A, (suc_lll)
    OR   $48               ; H = $48 | LLL  (TT=1)
    LD   H, A
    LD   L, $20            ; source: char row 9 (RRR=1 within TT=1)
    LD   D, H
    LD   E, $00            ; dest: char row 8
    LD   BC, 224
    LDIR

    ; ── Cross TT=1→2: char row 16 → char row 15 ────────────────────────
    LD   A, (suc_lll)
    OR   $50               ; source H: TT=2, this LLL
    LD   H, A
    LD   L, $00
    LD   A, (suc_lll)
    OR   $48               ; dest H: TT=1, this LLL
    LD   D, A
    LD   E, $E0            ; dest: char row 15 (last row of TT=1)
    LD   BC, 32
    LDIR

    ; ── TT=2: copy char rows 17–23 → rows 16–22 (224 bytes) ────────────
    LD   A, (suc_lll)
    OR   $50               ; H = $50 | LLL  (TT=2)
    LD   H, A
    LD   L, $20
    LD   D, H
    LD   E, $00
    LD   BC, 224
    LDIR

    ; ── Clear char row 23 (TT=2, RRR=7) for this LLL ───────────────────
    LD   A, (suc_lll)
    OR   $50
    LD   H, A
    LD   L, $E0            ; RRR=7, L = 7×32 = $E0
    LD   B, 32
    XOR  A
suc_clr:
    LD   (HL), A
    INC  L
    DJNZ suc_clr

    ; ── Advance to next scan-line offset ────────────────────────────────
    LD   A, (suc_lll)
    INC  A
    LD   (suc_lll), A
    CP   8
    JR   NZ, suc_lll_loop
    RET

suc_lll: DB 0              ; current scan-line offset (0–7)

The outer loop runs eight times, once for each LLL value. Each pass executes three LDIR blocks of 224 bytes and two of 32 bytes, then clears 32 bytes — a total of 3×224 + 2×32 + 32 = 768 bytes processed per LLL, or 6144 bytes over the full loop. That matches the display-file size, confirming every pixel byte is visited exactly once.

Scrolling Attributes

The colour attribute file at $5800–$5AFF (768 bytes) is arranged in simple top-to-bottom, left-to-right order: 32 bytes per character row, 24 rows. ; scroll_up_char_attr
; Scrolls the colour attribute file ($5800–$5AFF) up by one character row.
; Attribute row 0 is lost; the new bottom row (attr row 23) is filled with
; zero (black ink, black paper, no flash, no bright).
; The pixel display file is not touched — call scroll_up_char separately.
; Destroys: A, B, C, D, E, H, L.
scroll_up_char_attr:
    LD   HL, $5820         ; source: attribute row 1
    LD   DE, $5800         ; dest:   attribute row 0
    LD   BC, 23 * 32       ; 23 rows × 32 bytes = 736 bytes
    LDIR
    LD   B, 32             ; clear the 32 bytes of attr row 23
    XOR  A
suca_clr:
    LD   (DE), A           ; DE points to attr row 23 after the LDIR
    INC  DE
    DJNZ suca_clr
    RET

Pixel-Level Shifting

A character-row scroll moves the display in 8-pixel jumps, which is visibly jerky. Smooth animation needs single-pixel granularity. Scrolling by one pixel row means copying each of the 192 pixel rows into the slot above it, then clearing the bottom row.

The relationship between consecutive pixel rows in memory is not constant. Within a character row, consecutive scan lines differ by one in the H register (same L): scan line 0 of char row 0 is at H = $40, scan line 1 at H = $41, and so on up to scan line 7 at H = $47. But the first scan line of character row 1 (visual pixel row 8) is at H = $40, L = $20 — H drops back to $40 while L advances by 32. This is the same cross-character-row step used by the sprite routines.

The routine below tracks two addresses simultaneously: DE is the destination (the row being overwritten) and HL is the source (the row one step below). After each 32-byte LDIR, the old source address becomes the new destination, and HL is advanced to the next pixel row using the same H/L step logic from the sprite drawing code.

scroll_up_pixel.asm

Save this file as lib/scroll_up_pixel.asm and add INCLUDE "lib/scroll_up_pixel.asm" to your program. Call it with CALL scroll_up_pixel — no input registers required.

; scroll_up_pixel
; Scrolls the entire display up by one pixel row (one scan line).
; Pixel row 0 is lost; the new bottom row (y=191) is filled with zeros.
; Destroys: A, B, C, D, E, H, L.
scroll_up_pixel:
    LD   DE, $4000         ; dest: pixel row 0  (TT=0, LLL=0, RRR=0)
    LD   HL, $4100         ; src:  pixel row 1  (TT=0, LLL=1, RRR=0)
    LD   B, 191            ; 191 copies: rows 1..191 → rows 0..190
sup_loop:
    PUSH BC                ; save row counter — LDIR overwrites BC
    PUSH HL                ; save source start — LDIR advances HL
    LD   BC, 32
    LDIR                   ; copy 32 bytes: source row → dest row
    POP  HL                ; restore source start address
    LD   D, H
    LD   E, L              ; old source becomes next iteration's dest
    ; Advance HL to the next pixel row using the ZX Spectrum row-step logic
    LD   A, H
    AND  $07               ; isolate LLL (scan-line offset field)
    CP   $07
    JR   Z, sup_cross
    INC  H                 ; LLL < 7: step scan-line field in H
    JR   sup_dec
sup_cross:
    LD   A, H
    AND  $F8               ; LLL = 7: clear scan-line field (→ LLL=0)
    LD   H, A
    LD   A, L
    ADD  A, $20            ; step RRR (character-row field in L)
    LD   L, A
    JR   NC, sup_dec
    LD   A, H
    ADD  A, $08            ; carry: step TT (screen-third field in H)
    LD   H, A
sup_dec:
    POP  BC
    DJNZ sup_loop

    ; After the loop DE points to the start of pixel row 191.
    LD   H, D
    LD   L, E
    LD   B, 32
    XOR  A
sup_clr:
    LD   (HL), A
    INC  L
    DJNZ sup_clr
    RET

Why HL Starts at $4100

Pixel row 0 is at H = $40, L = $00. Pixel row 1 — the first scan line of the same character row, one step down — is at H = $41, L = $00, i.e. address $4100. The first LDIR therefore copies 32 bytes from $4100 to $4000, sliding the visual second row into the first slot. After the copy, HL is restored to $4100, set as the new DE, and then advanced to pixel row 2 ($4200) by a single INC H.

The crossing case occurs seven times per character row, each time at pixel row 8k−1 where LLL = 7. For example, after copying pixel row 7 ($4700) into pixel row 6 ($4600), HL holds $4700 and the advance logic clears LLL, adds $20 to L (stepping RRR from 0 to 1), and produces $4020 — the address of pixel row 8 (char row 1, scan line 0). That is exactly the same step performed by the sprite drawing routines.

Speed Comparison

scroll_up_char processes all 6144 display bytes in one call and the loop runs only 8 times. scroll_up_pixel also processes 6144 bytes (191×32 = 6112 plus the 32-byte clear), but the overhead of 191 PUSH/POP pairs and 191 row-advance computations is roughly 25× higher. On a 3.5 MHz machine, a character-row scroll completes in under 2 ms; a pixel-row scroll takes around 6–8 ms. Both are fast enough to run every frame at 50 Hz, but the pixel-row version leaves less time for game logic.

Smooth Scrolling Techniques

Horizontal Scrolling

Vertical scrolling moves the screen up or down by shifting data between scan-line addresses. Horizontal scrolling moves every pixel left or right, which means rotating bits within each row rather than copying bytes between rows.

Z80 provides the RR (rotate right through carry) instruction that rotates a register or memory location one bit to the right: the existing carry flag enters bit 7 (the leftmost pixel), and old bit 0 exits into the carry flag. Applying RR (HL) to each byte from left to right with carry cleared before the first byte creates a right-scroll: bit 0 of each byte flows into bit 7 of the next, carrying pixels across byte boundaries. The carry initially set to zero injects a blank pixel at the left edge.

scroll_right_pixel.asm

Save this file as lib/scroll_right_pixel.asm and add INCLUDE "lib/scroll_right_pixel.asm" to your program. Call it with CALL scroll_right_pixel — no input registers required.

; scroll_right_pixel
; Shifts the entire display one pixel to the right.
; Pixels flow across byte boundaries via the carry flag.
; A zero pixel is injected at column 0 of each row.
; Destroys: A, B, D, H, L.
scroll_right_pixel:
    LD   HL, $4000         ; start of display
    LD   D, 192            ; 192 pixel rows
srp_row:
    PUSH HL                ; save row start for the advance step below
    OR   A                 ; clear carry: inject 0 pixel at left edge
    LD   B, 32             ; 32 bytes per row
srp_byte:
    RR   (HL)              ; CF → bit 7 (left), old bit 0 → CF
    INC  L
    DJNZ srp_byte
    POP  HL                ; restore row start
    ; Advance HL to the next pixel row
    LD   A, H
    AND  $07
    CP   $07
    JR   Z, srp_cross
    INC  H
    JR   srp_dec
srp_cross:
    LD   A, H
    AND  $F8
    LD   H, A
    LD   A, L
    ADD  A, $20
    LD   L, A
    JR   NC, srp_dec
    LD   A, H
    ADD  A, $08
    LD   H, A
srp_dec:
    DEC  D
    JR   NZ, srp_row
    RET

scroll_left_pixel.asm

Save this file as lib/scroll_left_pixel.asm and add INCLUDE "lib/scroll_left_pixel.asm" to your program. Call it with CALL scroll_left_pixel — no input registers required.

For a left scroll, RL (rotate left through carry: old bit 7 → carry, carry → bit 0) is used instead of RR, and each row is processed from right to left so the carry flows in the correct direction: old bit 7 of byte k+1 (leftmost pixel of the next byte) becomes new bit 0 of byte k (rightmost pixel of the current byte).

; scroll_left_pixel
; Shifts the entire display one pixel to the left.
; Pixels flow across byte boundaries via the carry flag.
; A zero pixel is injected at column 255 of each row.
; Destroys: A, B, D, H, L.
scroll_left_pixel:
    LD   HL, $4000         ; start of display
    LD   D, 192            ; 192 pixel rows
slp_row:
    PUSH HL                ; save row start for the advance step below
    LD   A, L
    ADD  A, 31             ; point to the last byte of this row
    LD   L, A
    OR   A                 ; clear carry: inject 0 pixel at right edge
    LD   B, 32             ; 32 bytes per row
slp_byte:
    RL   (HL)              ; CF → bit 0 (right), old bit 7 → CF
    DEC  L
    DJNZ slp_byte
    POP  HL                ; restore row start
    ; Advance HL to the next pixel row
    LD   A, H
    AND  $07
    CP   $07
    JR   Z, slp_cross
    INC  H
    JR   slp_dec
slp_cross:
    LD   A, H
    AND  $F8
    LD   H, A
    LD   A, L
    ADD  A, $20
    LD   L, A
    JR   NC, slp_dec
    LD   A, H
    ADD  A, $08
    LD   H, A
slp_dec:
    DEC  D
    JR   NZ, slp_row
    RET

Note that OR A clears the carry flag as a side effect (it always resets CF). AND A also works. Both are one byte and four T-states, making them the standard idiom for clearing carry on the Z80.

Avoiding Tearing

The Spectrum's ULA refreshes the screen continuously at 50 Hz. If the CPU is writing to the display file while the electron beam is drawing those bytes, the visible result is a horizontal tear — part of the frame shows the old pixels, part shows the new. Tearing is most noticeable with large scroll operations.

The safe window for display updates is the vertical blank, the period after the beam has finished the last visible line (y = 191) and before it starts the next frame at y = 0. On the Spectrum this window lasts roughly 1.25 ms. The standard way to synchronise to it is to hook the maskable interrupt: the ULA fires an interrupt request at the start of every frame, so an IM 1 or IM 2 handler that performs the scroll is guaranteed to run during the vertical blank. A simpler but less precise alternative is to count T-states and start the scroll at the calculated frame boundary.

For demos where a small amount of tearing is acceptable, the fastest approach is simply to call the scroll routine as quickly as possible and let the timing fall where it may. At 50 fps the tear moves up or down the screen each frame and blends into the motion.

Combining Scroll with Sprites

A common game pattern is a scrolling background with sprites moving over it. The simplest approach:

  1. Erase each sprite at its current position (using draw_sprite_masked with an all-zero erase sprite, as in the invader demo).
  2. Call scroll_up_pixel or scroll_up_char to advance the background.
  3. Redraw each sprite at its new position.

This works cleanly because the masked erase only touches the sprite's bounding box; the scroll only touches the background behind it. As long as step 1 precedes step 2, the erase clears the correct pixels before they are shifted, and the redraw in step 3 places the sprite correctly against the new background.

Scroll Speed

Calling scroll_up_pixel once per frame (50 Hz) gives a scrolling speed of 50 pixels per second — smooth but slow. To scroll faster, call it multiple times per frame or switch to scroll_up_char for 8-pixel steps. Alternatively, mix the two: call scroll_up_char once per frame for 400 pixels/second, or hold a sub-pixel counter and call scroll_up_pixel N times on frames where the accumulated offset requires it.

A practical rule of thumb: if the scroll speed in pixels per frame is a multiple of 8, use scroll_up_char for maximum efficiency. For any other speed, use scroll_up_pixel and accept the extra CPU cost. On a 3.5 MHz machine, even scroll_up_pixel consumes less than 25% of a frame's T-states, leaving ample time for sprite logic and game state updates.

Complete Example: scroll_demo.asm

This program exercises all five Chapter 7 scroll routines against an animated striped background. Alternating solid yellow and black character rows are drawn once at startup; holding different number keys then switches the active scroll routine so the contrast between pixel-level and character-row scrolling is immediately visible.

Build with: pasmo --tapbas scroll_demo.asm scroll_demo.tap

Why there is no sprite in this demo

Keeping a sprite visually fixed on a scrolling background seems straightforward: erase the sprite, scroll the background, redraw the sprite at the same position. The problem is timing. Each of the Chapter 7 scroll routines must touch every one of the 6 144 bytes in the display file, which at 3.5 MHz takes between 20 ms (scroll_up_char) and 60 ms (scroll_left_pixel). A PAL ZX Spectrum redraws its screen every 20 ms. The scroll routine alone therefore spans at least one complete display refresh — and during the whole of that time the sprite has been erased but not yet redrawn. The electron beam sweeps through the sprite's row while the pixels are black, producing a visible flash on every single frame.

The standard solution is double buffering: draw the complete next frame — background and sprite together — into a second block of memory that is not currently being displayed, then copy it to the visible display in one fast burst during the vertical blanking interval. Because the copy happens while the beam is off-screen, the viewer never sees a partial frame. Double buffering is covered in full in Chapter 9.

Background and Initialisation

draw_background fills the display with horizontal stripes by iterating through the 24 character rows. For each row it computes three values stored in memory variables — the fill byte, the base H value, and the starting L value — then runs a small inner loop to fill all eight scan lines. The two pieces of arithmetic are worth noting:

  • B AND $18 (= AND %00011000) extracts bits 4:3 of the character row index, which equals TT × 8 directly, avoiding any division. OR $40 builds the complete H_base in one further instruction.
  • Five consecutive ADD A, A instructions multiply RRR by 32, computing L_start (the column-0 address of the character row) without needing a shift loop or lookup table.
; draw_background
; Fills the display with horizontal stripes: even character rows all $FF
; (all pixels = ink colour), odd rows all $00 (all pixels = paper colour).
draw_background:
    LD   B, 0              ; B = character row index (0..23)
db_next_row:
    LD   A, B
    AND  1
    JR   NZ, db_is_odd
    LD   A, $FF
    JR   db_have_val
db_is_odd:
    XOR  A
db_have_val:
    LD   (db_val), A

    ; H_base = $40 | (TT<<3).  B AND $18 gives TT<<3 for B in 0..23.
    LD   A, B
    AND  $18               ; bits 4:3 of B = TT<<3  (0, 8, or 16)
    OR   $40
    LD   (db_hbase), A

    ; L_start = (B AND 7) << 5 = RRR * 32
    LD   A, B
    AND  $07
    ADD  A, A
    ADD  A, A
    ADD  A, A
    ADD  A, A
    ADD  A, A               ; five doublings: A = RRR << 5
    LD   (db_lstart), A

    LD   C, 0              ; C = LLL (0..7), reset for each new character row
db_scan:
    LD   A, (db_hbase)
    ADD  A, C              ; H = H_base + LLL
    LD   H, A
    LD   A, (db_lstart)
    LD   L, A
    LD   A, (db_val)
    PUSH BC                ; DJNZ overwrites B; save B=char-row and C=LLL
    LD   B, 32
db_fill:
    LD   (HL), A
    INC  L
    DJNZ db_fill
    POP  BC                ; restore B=char-row, C=LLL
    INC  C
    LD   A, C
    CP   8
    JR   NZ, db_scan

    INC  B
    LD   A, B
    CP   24
    JR   NZ, db_next_row
    RET

db_val:    DB 0
db_hbase:  DB 0
db_lstart: DB 0

The Main Loop

The main loop checks for SPACE (exit), then reads the held key and calls the appropriate scroll routine. A short software delay paces the loop to roughly 10 frames per second so the motion is easy to follow.

main_loop:
    ; Exit on SPACE
    LD   BC, $7FFE
    IN   A, (C)
    AND  $01
    RET  Z

    ; Key 6 → scroll_up_char (8-pixel jump) + attribute scroll
    LD   BC, $EFFE
    IN   A, (C)
    BIT  4, A             ; bit = 0 means key is held
    JR   NZ, not_key6
    CALL scroll_up_char
    CALL scroll_up_char_attr
    JR   after_scroll
not_key6:
    ; Key 5 → scroll_left_pixel
    LD   BC, $F7FE
    IN   A, (C)
    BIT  4, A
    JR   NZ, not_key5
    CALL scroll_left_pixel
    JR   after_scroll
not_key5:
    ; Key 8 → scroll_right_pixel
    LD   BC, $EFFE
    IN   A, (C)
    BIT  2, A
    JR   NZ, not_key8
    CALL scroll_right_pixel
    JR   after_scroll
not_key8:
    ; Default: scroll_up_pixel (smooth 1-pixel-per-frame upward scroll)
    CALL scroll_up_pixel
after_scroll:
    CALL demo_delay
    JP   main_loop

The keyboard uses the ZX Spectrum half-row port system: port $EFFE covers the number keys 6–0, port $F7FE covers 1–5. Within each port, bits are active low — a zero bit means the key is currently held. BIT n, A sets the Z flag if bit n is zero, so JR NZ skips the scroll call when the key is not pressed, and falls through when it is.

Observing scroll_up_char vs scroll_up_pixel

Release all keys (or hold any key other than 5, 6, or 8): the background glides upward one pixel per frame, the stripe boundaries shifting smoothly and continuously. Hold key 6: each frame jumps the display by a full character row (8 pixels). The yellow bar nearest the top disappears in one instant rather than fading out across eight frames — the distinctive staircase of coarse scrolling. The paired call to scroll_up_char_attr keeps the attribute colours locked to the stripe positions; without it the colours drift out of alignment after the first press.

Observing scroll_left_pixel and scroll_right_pixel

Hold key 5 or 8. The horizontal stripes lose their uniformity within a few frames: each row's bytes shift independently, so a yellow byte scrolling off one edge wraps back as a black byte at the other, gradually mixing the two colours. This is a good visual reminder that the scroll routines treat the display as a flat array of bytes — they have no concept of stripes, sprites, or any other higher-level structure.

What You See

The display opens with 12 solid yellow bars alternating with 12 black gaps, each 8 pixels tall. By default the background scrolls up one pixel per frame, the bars flowing continuously toward the top and wrapping in from the bottom. Holding 6 produces the same net motion but in visible 8-pixel jumps. Holding 5 or 8 rotates the display sideways, gradually fragmenting the clean stripes into a mixture of yellow and black as the rows wrap around.
